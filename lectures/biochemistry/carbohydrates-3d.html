D-Glucose (Open-Chain Aldohexose)

C₆H₁₂O₆

A 6-carbon polyhydroxy aldehyde (aldose). Contains 4 chiral centers (C2, C3, C4, C5), giving 2⁴ = 16 stereoisomers. In aqueous solution, <1% exists in this open-chain form.

1

Definition & Biological Importance

Chemical Definition: Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that yield these compounds upon hydrolysis. Many simpler carbohydrates follow the empirical formula (CH₂O)ₙ (where n ≥ 3), historically giving rise to the name "hydrates of carbon."

Key Roles in Living Organisms (Source & Literature)

Primary Energy Source

Most abundant organic molecules in nature. Synthesized by plants via photosynthesis and oxidized by cells (glycolysis & citric acid cycle) to generate ATP (~4 kcal/g).

Energy Storage Reserves

Stored compactly as insoluble polymers: Starch (amylose & amylopectin) in plants and Glycogen in animal liver and skeletal muscles.

Structural Integrity

Form load-bearing frameworks: Cellulose microfibrils in plant cell walls, Chitin in arthropod exoskeletons/fungi, and Peptidoglycan in bacteria.

Genetic & Cellular Signaling

Ribose & Deoxyribose form the backbone of RNA, DNA, ATP, and coenzymes (NAD⁺, FAD, CoA). Glycoproteins/glycolipids act as cell-surface antigens (e.g., ABO blood groups).

2

Types & Classification

Carbohydrates are classified into three major groups based on the number of sugar units produced upon acid or enzymatic hydrolysis:

A. Monosaccharides ("Simple Sugars")

Single polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone units that cannot be hydrolyzed into simpler carbohydrates. Classified by carbonyl functional group (Aldose if terminal aldehyde -CHO; Ketose if internal ketone C=O) and carbon count:

Carbons Category Aldose Example Ketose Example
3 Trioses Glyceraldehyde Dihydroxyacetone
4 Tetroses Erythrose, Threose Erythrulose
5 Pentoses Ribose, Xylose Ribulose, Xylulose
6 Hexoses Glucose, Galactose, Mannose Fructose
7 / 9 Heptoses / Nonoses Glucoheptose Sedoheptulose / Neuraminic acid

B. Oligosaccharides (2–10 Monosaccharide Units)

Short chains of monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds (defined as 2–6 units in introductory texts, up to 10 in IUPAC nomenclature). Disaccharides (2 units) are the most abundant—e.g., Sucrose, Lactose, Maltose, Isomaltose. Trisaccharides (e.g., Raffinose) and tetrasaccharides (e.g., Stachyose) occur in legumes.

C. Polysaccharides (Glycans: >10 to Thousands of Units)

1. Homopolysaccharides

Composed of a single type of monomer unit. Examples: Starch, Glycogen, Cellulose, Chitin, Inulin.

2. Heteropolysaccharides

Composed of two or more different monomer types. Examples: Glycosaminoglycans (Hyaluronic acid, Heparin), Peptidoglycan, Agar.

Chemical Structure & Stereoisomerism

Asymmetric Carbons & D/L Configuration

A carbon atom bonded to 4 different groups is asymmetric (chiral). A molecule with n chiral centers has 2ⁿ stereoisomers (e.g., aldohexoses have 4 chiral centers → 16 optical isomers).

  • Enantiomers: Non-superimposable mirror images (e.g., D-Glyceraldehyde vs. L-Glyceraldehyde).
  • D vs. L Assignment: Determined by the chiral carbon farthest from the carbonyl group (C5 in glucose). If -OH is on the right in a Fischer projection, it is a D-sugar (predominant in nature); if on the left, it is an L-sugar.
  • Epimers: Diastereomers differing in configuration at only one chiral carbon (e.g., D-Glucose and D-Galactose are C4 epimers; D-Glucose and D-Mannose are C2 epimers).

Cyclization: Pyranoses, Furanoses & Mutarotation

In aqueous solution, monosaccharides with 5 or more carbons react intramolecularly: an alcohol -OH attacks the carbonyl carbon to form a cyclic hemiacetal (from aldose) or hemiketal (from ketose):

Pyranose (6-Membered Ring)

5 carbons + 1 oxygen (e.g., C5-OH attacks C1 aldehyde in D-Glucose → Glucopyranose).

Furanose (5-Membered Ring)

4 carbons + 1 oxygen (e.g., C5-OH attacks C2 ketone in D-Fructose → Fructofuranose).

Anomers (α vs. β) & Mutarotation: Cyclization turns the carbonyl carbon into a new chiral center called the anomeric carbon. In Haworth projections of D-sugars, the α-anomer has the anomeric -OH pointing down (trans to C6), while the β-anomer has it pointing up (cis to C6). Spontaneous interconversion between forms in water is called mutarotation (D-glucose equilibrates at ~36% α, ~63% β, and <1% open-chain).

Chemical Bonds in Carbohydrates

1. O-Glycosidic Bond (C–O–C Linkage)

Condensation (-H₂O)

A covalent acetal/ketal bond formed between the anomeric carbon of one sugar (hemiacetal) and a hydroxyl oxygen (-OH) of another molecule with the elimination of water (H₂O). Cleaved via hydrolysis.

α(1→4) Bond: Linear chains in maltose, amylose, and glycogen. Digestible by human α-amylase.
α(1→6) Bond: Creates branch points in amylopectin (every 24–30 units) and glycogen (every 8–12 units).
β(1→4) Bond: Found in lactose and cellulose. Requires lactase or microbial cellulase to hydrolyze.

2. N-Glycosidic Bond (C–N Linkage)

Formed between the anomeric carbon of a carbohydrate (like D-ribose or 2-deoxy-D-ribose) and the nitrogen atom of an amine. Essential for linking nitrogenous bases (purines/pyrimidines) to pentose sugars in nucleotides (DNA, RNA, ATP) and in N-linked glycoproteins.

3. Hydrogen Bonds

Extensive inter-chain H-bonding between parallel β(1→4) glucan chains bundles cellulose into rigid, water-insoluble microfibrils. Also stabilizes helical coils in amylose.

4. Phosphoester Bonds

Formed when a hydroxyl group (e.g., C6 of glucose) reacts with phosphoric acid (e.g., Glucose-6-phosphate), trapping sugars inside cells during glycolysis.

Key Biological Examples

Major Disaccharides (Reducing vs. Non-Reducing)

Disaccharide Subunits & Linkage Source Reducing?
Maltose α-D-Glc-(1→4)-D-Glc Malt, starch digestion Yes (free C1)
Lactose β-D-Gal-(1→4)-D-Glc Mammalian milk Yes (free C1)
Isomaltose α-D-Glc-(1→6)-D-Glc Amylopectin branch hydrolysis Yes (free C1)
Sucrose α-D-Glc-(1↔2)-β-D-Fru Sugarcane, sugar beet No (both C1 & C2 locked)

Major Polysaccharides

Starch (Amylose + Amylopectin)

Plant energy storage. Amylose is unbranched α(1→4) D-glucose forming a left-handed helix (~6 residues/turn). Amylopectin is branched via α(1→6) links.

Glycogen ("Animal Starch")

Animal glucose reserve. Structurally similar to amylopectin (α1→4 chains) but much more compact and heavily branched (α1→6 every 8–12 residues) for rapid mobilization.

Cellulose

Most abundant organic polymer on Earth. Linear β(1→4) D-glucose chains where every alternate glucose is flipped 180°, creating straight fibers for plant cell walls.

Chitin & Glycosaminoglycans

Chitin: Homopolymer of β(1→4) N-acetylglucosamine in arthropods/fungi. GAGs: Negatively charged heteropolysaccharides (e.g., Hyaluronic acid) in extracellular matrix.
